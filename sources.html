These are general reference articles, written for a broad audience and openly readable. They agree on the statements this site makes. Where a claim is a specific number, it is an arithmetic consequence of the definitions given in [1] and [2], worked out on the page rather than copied from a source.

  1. Thévenin's theorem. Wikipedia. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Th%C3%A9venin%27s_theorem

    Statement of the theorem; Vth as the open-circuit voltage; Rth by zeroing independent sources or as Vth/Isc; the test-source method for dependent sources; practical limitations; the 1853 Helmholtz and 1883 Thévenin attributions.

  2. Maximum power transfer theorem. Wikipedia. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_power_transfer_theorem

    Maximum power when load resistance equals source resistance; the 1840 Jacobi attribution; the distinction between maximum power and maximum efficiency; the 50% efficiency at the match.

  3. Linear circuit. Wikipedia. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_circuit

    Definition of a linear circuit by superposition; why linearity is the condition for a two-number terminal model.

  4. Superposition principle. Wikipedia. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superposition_theorem

    The superposition principle for linear systems; the basis for zeroing one independent source at a time.

  5. Dependent source. Wikipedia. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dependent_source

    Definition of a dependent (controlled) source as one whose value depends on a voltage or current elsewhere; examples such as the transistor and op-amp models.

  6. Ohm's law. Wikipedia. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohm%27s_law

    The relation V = I R used in every calculation on this site.

  7. Kirchhoff's circuit laws. Wikipedia. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirchhoff%27s_circuit_laws

    KVL and KCL, used in the worked examples to solve for Vth.

A caution. These are reference articles, not the original papers or a textbook. The theorem statements are standard and stable, but if you need a citable source for coursework, follow each article's own reference list to the underlying texts.

Dates and attributions stated on this site (Helmholtz 1853, Thévenin 1883, Jacobi around 1840) come from [1] and [2].
