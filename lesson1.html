A circuit has a view from its terminals

Two terminals are all a load can see

Every circuit you will build has a part that does the work — the source and its network — and a part that uses it — the load (a resistor, a sensor, a meter, the next stage). The two are joined at a pair of terminals. From the load's side, everything to the left of those terminals is a black box. It does not matter which resistor is where inside: the box presents a voltage across the terminals and lets a current flow out of them.

Thévenin's theorem is the statement that this black box can be replaced, for every purpose that only involves the terminals, by the simplest possible circuit:[1]

Vth in series with Rth

where Vth is the open-circuit voltage the box shows when nothing is connected, and Rth is the resistance as seen from the terminals once every independent source inside is set to zero.[1]

Why this is allowed

A linear network obeys superposition and Ohm's law everywhere.[3][4] Its terminal behaviour is therefore a straight line: the terminal voltage falls as the load draws more current,

V = Vth − I · Rth

A straight line is fixed by two numbers. They are exactly Vth (the voltage at zero current) and Rth (the drop per unit of current). Any two-component circuit that matches those two numbers behaves identically at the terminals, so we may as well use it.

The equivalent is defined at the terminals

Read the phrase carefully: the equivalent is valid as seen from the terminals you chose. Move the terminals, and you get a different equivalent.

Where the idea stops working

The theorem needs a linear circuit. Resistors, independent sources, and the ideal op-amp are fine. Diodes, transistors driven hard, and anything else with a curved V–I characteristic are not: their terminal curve is not a straight line, so two numbers cannot capture it.[1][3]

Three more cautions, all of which cost marks:

  • Independence is required for the shortcut. When you set sources to zero to find Rth, you may only zero independent sources. A dependent (controlled) source stays, and you must find Rth another way — Lesson 2 shows how.[5]
  • Nothing inside is preserved. The equivalent tells you nothing about the voltage across an internal resistor or the power it dissipates. Use it only for what happens at the terminals.
  • Not the same as the source. Vth is rarely the value printed on the source; Rth is rarely one of the resistors you started with. They are the combined effect.

A ten-resistor network and a one-source-one-resistor circuit both show 6 V open-circuit and 2 Ω when you look into the same two terminals. In what sense are they the same circuit, and in what sense are they not?

Check yourself

A 12 V source feeds a 4 Ω resistor, and you have chosen your terminals across that resistor. What is Vth?

The open-circuit voltage is the voltage across the 4 Ω when nothing is attached to the terminals. No current flows through the external path, but the source still drives its own loop, so the resistor carries 12 V ÷ 4 Ω = 3 A, and the terminal voltage is 3 A × 4 Ω = 12 V. Here Vth happens to equal the source voltage, which is why “the source is the Thévenin voltage” is a common but unreliable shortcut.

Two circuits have the same Vth = 6 V and Rth = 2 Ω as seen from their terminals, but one hides a 100 Ω resistor inside. Are they equivalent?

Yes — as seen from the terminals. Any load connected there cannot tell them apart. The difference shows up only in quantities inside the box, such as the current through the hidden resistor, which the equivalent cannot reproduce. Equivalence is a statement about the terminals.
