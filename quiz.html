1. In one or two sentences, what is a Thévenin equivalent?

Show answer

It is the replacement of any linear network, as seen from a chosen pair of terminals, by a single voltage source Vth in series with a single resistor Rth. The two components reproduce the network's terminal behaviour exactly for every load. [1]

2. For a network with Vth = 10 V and Rth = 5 Ω, predict the terminal voltage and the current when a 5 Ω load is attached.

Show answer

The load and Rth are equal, so they split the source voltage evenly:

IL = 10 V / (5 Ω + 5 Ω) = 1 A, VL = 1 A × 5 Ω = 5 V

Half of Vth appears across the load. This is the matched case of Lesson 3, so the load is also taking maximum power. [1][2]

3. Why can you not find Rth by replacing the sources with shorts and opens when a dependent source is present?

Show answer

A dependent source has no fixed value of its own; its value is set by a voltage or current elsewhere in the network. Zeroing it would change the very quantities that control it, so the circuit you analyse would not be the one you were given. Only independent sources may be zeroed; with a dependent source you must use the short-circuit method or a test source instead. [1][5]

4. A source has Vth = 2 V and Rth = 50 Ω, and it is connected to a 50 Ω load. Is this the maximum-power design? What efficiency results, and when would you deliberately choose a much larger load instead?

Show answer

Yes — the load equals Rth, so it receives maximum power, Pmax = Vth²/(4Rth) = (2)²/(4 × 50) = 20 mW. The efficiency, however, is only 50%: the other half of the source power is lost in Rth. You accept that when the signal is weak and you must capture every microwatt (an antenna or sensor). For a power system you would choose RL ≫ Rth, which lowers the delivered power but raises the efficiency. [2]

5. Find Rth at a–b for this network: a 12 V source feeds a 3 Ω resistor, then a 6 Ω resistor to ground; the terminals a–b are across the 6 Ω resistor.

Show answer

No dependent sources, so zero the 12 V source (a short). Looking into a–b, the 3 Ω and 6 Ω resistors are now in parallel:

Rth = 3 Ω ∥ 6 Ω = (3 × 6)/(3 + 6) = 2 Ω

For reference, the open-circuit voltage is Vth = 12 V × 6/(3 + 6) = 8 V. [1][6]
